That identification matters beyond bookkeeping, because OLS is what the covered-call literature reports and therefore what II-21's comparison needs.

  **Down-beta is exactly 1.000, in every configuration, and it is analytic.** A lot still in inventory is a lot below its call strike, and below the strike a covered call is pure stock: the payoff change equals the underlying's for every down move, so both slope and intercept are exact. **That is the claim most often made for covered calls — that they cushion a fall — refuted inside the article's own machinery, without data.** The premium is a cushion worth one period's premium against the whole decline, and the model prices it at exactly that.

  **And inventory is not the whole book, which is where the sharper sentence is.** Shocking the whole book — including the live short put — and marking it gives, per unit of capital: **1.07 at −20%, 0.93 undisturbed, 0.61 at +20%.** Almost exactly one full share of the downside excess is the put, whose delta runs to +1 as it goes into the money. **The wheel is more than fully exposed on the way down and less than fully exposed on the way up.** The ladder also exhibits the reversal directly rather than as a regression coefficient, which is what the item asked be kept beside the betas.

  **The lever is n, and it is §07's grid tax arriving a second time.** Up-beta runs 0.93 / 0.83 / 0.68 at n = 1, 4, 13 and moves by 0.004 between the strike regimes. Freezing a strike for four put periods cost holding time in §07; here the same freeze costs upside, for the same reason. Higher volatility *reduces* the asymmetry (0.87 at σ = 30%), because lots run deeper and their calls sit further out. [Israelov & Nielsen (2015)](#ref:israelov-nielsen-2015)'s term **equity reversal** is adopted, with their attribution of ~25% of a covered call's risk to it at a Sharpe near 0.10 — a real risk paid almost nothing, which the model independently assigns no return because under its price process such an exposure cannot be compensated.

- ✅ **The sensitivity sweep, and the two kinds of parameter it separates** (TODO II-7; 2026-07-29). `sensitivity()` in `model.py`, with `_sweep_cell`/`_sweep_job`/`_sweep_axes` beside it and `stationary_converged()` up with the numerics; printed by `main()` by default, skipped with `--no-sweep`. The item's own question — fold into II-5's report, or become the sweep that needs the pool — is answered by the second: γ_s, A and ε never touch the depth walk and σ, μ, p\*, n and T all do, so every cell is its own stationary solve. 38 of them, one pool round, 6.7 s → 21.8 s for the whole script and byte-identical to `WHEEL_WORKERS=1`.

  **The result is that the parameters sort into two kinds that behave nothing alike.** The dials an operator *chooses* are benign and near-linear: A\* goes **exactly as 1/T** along the cadence (an identity — λ ∝ 1/T with the walk and E[W] untouched — now pinned in `verify_examples.py`), nearly as λ along p\* (4.50 / 9.25 / **19.23** / 35.43 at p\* = 5/10/20/35%), and sub-linearly in the call length (12.11 / 15.02 / **19.23** / 25.35 / 31.22 at n = 1/2/4/8/13). The two an operator only *estimates* are not: A\* runs **7.97 / 19.23 / 48.97 / >126** across σ = 15/20/25/28% and diverges at 30%, and **3.74 / 6.81 / 19.23 / none at all** down μ = 13/10/7/4%. The elasticity d ln A\*/d ln σ is **4.2** at the running example, so a 1% relative error in the volatility estimate is a 4% error in the equity required, and it is unbounded as the boundary is approached. Two stocks a practitioner would describe identically — "a quality name around 20 vol" — differ by **2.5×** in the equity their wheels need if one of them is actually at 25.

  **The compounding, which was not anticipated.** Both terms of A\* = E[I(∞)]/L_max move the same way: survivable leverage collapses toward 1 exactly where inventory demand explodes. The equity discount the broker's permission buys — II-5's 12% at the running example — is **35% at σ = 15% and 0.4% at σ = 25%**. Leverage stops helping precisely where capital is scarcest, which is the sharpest form yet of "capacity comes from equity".

  **The operational column.** Throughput and T_sat are read at one fixed account, the running example's own A\* = 19.23, so they answer "what does an operator who sized for this stock get on that one": **39.3% throughput and saturation in 29.9 years at σ = 25%**, 15.3% and 23.4y at σ = 28%. That is III-1's "sizing against mean capital is precisely the mistake" in its second form, and arguably the worse one — the mistake is not only that demand is bursty around its mean, it is that **the mean itself is a near-singular function of two parameters nobody knows exactly**.

  **The (σ, μ) plane is where the two stability boundaries are curves.** 16 cells, A\* alone, `never` where ν ≤ 0 and `*` where the count is stationary but the capital integral is not — a band that opens at σ = 21.2% and is visibly wider than one would guess from the running example sitting inside it.

  **Two numerical findings, both of which had to be fixed before any number was quotable.** First, **`stationary()`'s defaults are sized for the running example and truncate off it**: the survival sum is closed with a geometric tail read off the last ten periods, which is exact only once the walk has settled into its slowest mode, and settling takes of order σ²/ν² years — 64 at the running example against 2,300 at σ = 28%. At ν = +0.004 the default cap of 8000 periods reads E[I] = 93.5 against 104.8; at σ = 28% it reads 106 against 118 at twice the cap and 129 at eight times. `stationary_converged()` doubles the cap until the walk dies or a doubling moves E[J] by less than 1%, and **reports cells it could not converge as lower bounds** (`>`) rather than quoting a truncation. The independent read on those is Siegmund's E[T] ≈ (E[x₀] + βσ√τ_c)/ν, which converged cells exceed by 5–12% across the whole sweep and truncated ones fall *below* — the σ = 28% cell reads 1.05 and is marked. **Nothing the article quotes moves**: the running example is converged inside the old default, which is now pinned as a check, and `stationary()` was left exactly as it was.

  Second, **ν = 0 is not a value the grid rule can be handed**. x_max is sized by the same θ·x_max > 8 rule `report()` applies to the capital integral, and σ = 30% at μ = 7% is ν = 0 exactly — which floating point delivers as **+6.9e-18**, passing `count_ok`, asking for a grid of 4e18 cells and taking the process down with a `MemoryError` after several minutes of paging. Cells flatter than x_cap = 60 are now **refused rather than approximated**, printed as `~inf`, and the refusal is a regression check. This was found the expensive way: two full runs wedged before the cause was instrumented.

  **What `verify_examples.py` gained** (6.5 s → 8.6 s), under a structural heading since §11 is unwritten: the exact 1/T identity, the running example's insensitivity to the period cap, both verdicts of the converger (a walk that dies inside the cap, and a cap set too low reported as truncated), the boundary-cell refusal, and one pinned cell at the cheap end — A\* = 7.97 and L_max = 1.534 at σ = 15%.

  **Deliberately not swept.** Income and RoE: above σ = 21.2% the capital integral diverges while the lot count is still perfectly stationary, so every column is count-based and the divergence is flagged instead of quietly integrated. And the Q-world, since μ does not enter it — half the sweep would be a no-op, and the pricing measure's version of these figures is `frontier()`'s, already printed under every report's Q heading.

  **Amended 2026-07-29 (by II-13, when §11 came to quote it) — the elasticity is 3.5, and 4.2 is a secant labelled as a derivative.** This entry reads "the elasticity d ln A\*/d ln σ is **4.2** at the running example, so a 1% relative error in the volatility estimate is a 4% error in the equity required". - ✅ **The constrained simulator, and what it says the static barrier is wrong about** (TODO II-6; 2026-07-29). `wheel_sim.py --scenario constrained`. `Params` gains γ_s, A, u\*, the financing spread and a cash policy; `run_path` gains a ledger — cash and a debit, equity marked to market, blocking, and a continuously monitored liquidation barrier; `bridge_hit` is the crossing test; `batch_jobs`/`merge_all`/`simulate_batched` split a run over the pool without changing its answer. No formula is imported from the working-capital block: the barrier the simulator uses is its own ledger's, D/((1−γ_s)·I), and that it coincides with f\* is a result rather than a shared line of code.

  **The regression guard was met by diff, four ways.** `--scenario base|dividends|stress|sweep|validate` are **byte-identical** to their pre-change output, because `equity = inf` skips the ledger entirely rather than running it at an infinity; parallel matches `WHEEL_WORKERS=1` byte for byte in both the scenario and `verify_examples.py`; `model.py`'s own report is unchanged (the edits to it are comments); and `verify_examples.py` passes at `--full` and not, 4.4 s → 6.6 s.

  **Continuous monitoring, or the answer is about the grid.** Testing the barrier only at event dates misses every excursion that dips below and recovers inside a week, and by an amount that depends on the step. Each step therefore carries the Brownian-bridge crossing probability, exact conditional on its endpoints and hence **independent of the partition** — which is also what lets the shadow barrier below keep running on a coarser grid after the account is gone.

  **The paired construction, which is what makes a 1% probability measurable at four thousand paths.** From each path's first refused put — where the book is at capacity by construction, so realized leverage *is* the stopping rule's, no burn-in and no imported census — two barriers run side by side on the same price path and against the same uniform draw: the account's own, and that same barrier **frozen**, with the price alone moving it. The frozen one is `liquidation_prob`'s assumption measured rather than computed; the difference is paired.

  **model.py's closed forms are right about what they describe.** The frozen barrier reproduces `liquidation_prob` at every horizon and across two orders of magnitude of probability, the worst disagreement being 1.6 standard errors: **+0.0008 ± 0.0017** at 30y at the prudent account (against 0.0101), and **+0.0005 ± 0.0066 / −0.0009 ± 0.0075 / −0.0125 ± 0.0079** at 5/10/30y at L = 2 (against 0.2255 / 0.3400 / 0.4748). T_sat likewise: **18.9y simulated against 18.5y** at capacity 13.15, **8.6y against 8.8y** at capacity 10, with the whole unconstrained transient curve tracking — 5.31/7.93/10.76/13.40/15.56/18.78 against 5.20/7.90/10.50/13.49/15.31/18.23 lots at 2/5/10/20/30/60 years.

  **And what they describe is not an operator.** The same paths, with the account allowed to go on selling puts under its utilization rule, give **+0.0286 ± 0.0037** more liquidation at 30 years past saturation at the prudent account (3.95% against a frozen 1.09%, a factor of 3.6) and **+0.2520 ± 0.0084** at L = 2 (71.4% against 46.2%). The mechanism is a **ratchet**: a frozen book de-levers as the price rises, while an operator on a utilization rule buys instead, so the barrier follows the price up and does not follow it down. It is visible directly — the realized drift of ln(M/D), which the closed form takes to be ν − g = **+0.0250**, is **−0.0155 ± 0.0010**. This is II-13's "account migrating to its own boundary", sharpened: it does not migrate once, it re-migrates after every recovery, and the untended state is not a destination but a floor.

  **The net sign the item asked for is not a number, it is a function of the cash policy** — and that is the finding. At the running example, thirty years past saturation, against a frozen barrier that reads 0.78–1.09% under all four:

  | policy | realized g | d ln(E/S) | throughput | mean I | P(liq \| sat, 30y) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | retain everything | +4.00% | +2.19% | 77.7% | 13.19 | **0.35%** |
  | service the interest (g = 0) | +0.84% | −2.19% | 31.8% | 6.23 | 1.11% |
  | hold the account's size (draw 2.12%) | +3.46% | +0.12% | 61.4% | 10.64 | 3.95% |
  | withdraw the income (g = r_b) | +1.34% | −2.65% | 31.0% | 5.76 | **8.64%** |

  Full retention is **safer than the frozen barrier**; withdrawing the income is eight times worse than servicing it and twenty-five times worse than retaining, which is II-15's ranking confirmed in the currency it was predicted in.

  **The g linearization holds where it was derived and fails where it was extrapolated.** g = 0 realizes **+0.84%/yr** rather than 0 — small, and the debit really is close to a smooth exponential. g = r_b realizes **+1.34%** rather than +5.00%, a factor of 3.7, because withdrawing the income shrinks the account faster than the interest compounds the debit. The ranking survives; the arithmetic does not.

  **The frontier's cash policy is not the frontier's steady state, which is the largest single correction here.** A/A\* is a statement about an account of constant size in *shares*; g = 0 holds the debit constant in *dollars*, and a dollar-constant account shrinks against a compounding price. Simulated, the g = 0 policy **halves the throughput** — 31.8% against the frontier's 60.3% — and the account slides away from its capacity at 2.2%/yr. Staying stationary costs a draw of **r + econ_excess − m = 2.12% of equity per year**, derived rather than tuned and confirmed to **+0.0012 ± 0.0004/yr** of residual drift, against the **4.58–4.63%** `max_sustainable_draw` reports. Two figures both called sustainable, a factor of two apart, because they hold different things constant: one the liquidation risk, the other the business.

  **Uniform thinning survives in count and fails, mildly, in composition — and its composition error has the sign opposite to the one predicted.** Against a simulated unconstrained control over the same sixty years — not against the stationary census, which would charge blocking with the whole transient — the constrained book comes out **+0.7% in count, +0.4% in income and +0.7% in implied E[W]**. Under one per cent on every count-like quantity: thinning is a good approximation and the frontier's income and RoE figures rest on solid ground. The composition is where it bends, and only in the saturated regime: mean depth **0.5174 against the control's 0.4916, +5.3%**, with the deepest bin's share up 3.8 points; over the whole run, fill-up included, the same figure is 0.4868 against 0.4916 and the effect washes out. A blocked book is therefore **deeper** than a thinned one. II-13 predicted shallower, reasoning that blocking removes the lots that would have been bought cheapest. What blocking actually removes is the *newest* arrivals, and a new lot is a shallow lot; and for a near-unlevered account capacity in *lots* rises as the price falls, so blocking is if anything rarer during a drawdown. The stated expectation is withdrawn; see the amendment on II-13 in `TODO.md`.

  **One bug, caught before these figures were quoted, and worth recording because it is this project's documented failure mode.** The whole-run throughput was first computed as `1 − blocked/(sales + blocked)` when `sales` already counted every cadence date, refused or not. It understated the refusal rate, and — because that throughput is the factor the thinned control column is multiplied by — it manufactured a spurious **−8%** error in count, income and E[W] that read as a real finding and had a plausible story attached to it. Nothing else consumed it: the post-saturation throughput, the survival columns and the census shares all used correct denominators and did not move. The lesson is the old one in a new place — a ratio whose numerator and denominator are accumulated in different branches needs its denominator checked at the accumulation site, not at the point of use.

  **Two readings of survival, and only one of them is what an operator faces.** The closed form starts a saturated account; a real one is unlevered for its whole fill-up and cannot be sold out at all during it. From an empty book the prudent account's liquidation probability is **0.92% by 30 years and 12.15% by 60** — against 3.95% in the 30 years *after* saturation. Note the second figure against the ε = 10% the stopping rule was solved under for an **unbounded** horizon: allowed to keep buying, the account exceeds its own eventual-risk tolerance inside sixty years.

  **Integer lots are not a rounding detail at small account sizes.** At A = 5 the survivable rule permits 5.67 lots against equity of about 5.1 shares, so the book stops at 5 and the account **never borrows at all** — the permitted 13% leverage is unreachable, and the barrier is vacuous. That is why the levered testbed is A = 5 at L = 2 rather than at L_max. Even at A = 11.59 realized leverage after saturation is **0.745** against a stopping rule of 1.1349, because a book refilled one lot a week sits below its ceiling most of the time.

  **Five deviations from the item's brief, all forced by measurement.** (1) A fourth cash policy was added — a draw expressed as a fraction of equity — because *no* constant cash policy leaves the account stationary in the units `model.py` counts, and without one the census and throughput comparisons have no well-posed steady state to be about. (2) The census control is a simulated unconstrained run rather than the analytic stationary census, for the transient reason above. (3) The survival testbed is levered past survivable, since at 1.13× the barrier is an 84% drawdown and the resulting 1% probability, while measurable at the scenario's four thousand paths, is not at the few hundred `verify_examples.py` can afford. (4) After liquidation the price alone keeps being stepped, so the frozen shadow finishes instead of being censored by the live account's death — a bias of the same order as the effect. (5) Every drift diagnostic is restricted to surviving paths, since a liquidated path ends at the barrier by definition; where survival is low those columns are reported only as a ranking.

  **What `verify_examples.py` pins, and what it deliberately does not.** A seeded 384-path run at A = 5, L = 2 over 30 years, plus a 192-path unconstrained control: the unconstrained limit (no put refused, no barrier reached), T_sat against `time_to_inventory`, the frozen barrier against `liquidation_prob` **within 3 s.e. at every horizon**, and the live-versus-frozen correction pinned as a regression. Uniform thinning is **not** pinned there: at L = 2 most paths are liquidated inside the run, so a census read off the survivors is a survivorship artifact. That measurement is the prudent configuration's and belongs to the scenario.

Below A\* throughput retention is exactly **A/A\***, and realized leverage is exactly L_max — both identities, not fits, and both checked to 1e-12. - ✅ **The cash drawing rate, which enters survival as a displacement of the drift** (TODO II-15; raised and resolved 2026-07-29). `Config.draw` — cash withdrawn per year in share prices, negative for a deposit — plus `debit_growth()` and a private `_drift()`, and a `g` argument on `liquidation_prob` and `max_leverage`. No new machinery: the barrier, the distance a = −ln f\*, the reflection formula and the inversion are all unchanged.

  **The derivation.** Liquidation is a statement about the ratio of debit to market value, R = D/M. With inventory pinned at capacity, M moves only with the price, so ln M has drift ν and volatility σ; the debit compounds at the borrowing rate and is fed by the net drain, dD/dt = r_b·D + draw − income, so ln D has drift **g = r_b + (draw − income)/D**. Then ln R is Brownian with drift **g − ν** and volatility σ, started at ln(1 − 1/L) and absorbed at ln(1 − γ_s) — the same barrier, the same distance. Only the exponent moves:

        theta_eff  =  2(nu - g) / sigma^2.

  So every survival result reads ν − g where it read ν, and that is the whole change.

  **Verified independently, because the algebra was new and nothing else tests it.** A Monte Carlo carrying the price and the debit as *separate objects* — GBM for the book, exponential growth for the debit, liquidation checked as equity/market value crossing γ_s — agrees with the closed form at g = −1%, 0, +1%, +2%, at two resolutions: 400,000 paths over 400 years at yearly steps (diffs −0.00001, −0.00003, +0.00101, −0.00044 against 2 s.e. of 0.0006–0.0016), and 200,000 paths over 3,000 years at quarterly steps (+0.00041, −0.00049, +0.00033, +0.00053 against 2 s.e. of 0.0009–0.0022). Discrete monitoring would have undercounted crossings between samples, so each step carries the Brownian-bridge correction, exact conditional on the endpoints and drift-independent, which is what makes the step size a cost question rather than a bias one. Kept in the scratchpad, not `code/`; the durable check is `verify_examples.py`'s.

  **One trap the longer run walked into, worth keeping.** Compared against the *infinite*-horizon closed form the g = +2% cell reads −0.00985 and looks like a failure; compared against the closed form at the 3,000 years actually simulated, 0.62058, it reads +0.00053 and passes. At g = +2% the effective exponent is θ_eff = 0.25 — the Q-world's — and convergence to the infinite horizon is so slow that **three millennia is still visibly a finite horizon**. That is the same heavy tail [the stability section](#sec:stability) describes, met from the survival side, and it is the sharpest available illustration of why every survival figure must be quoted with its horizon.

  **The default is a policy, not the absence of one, and that is the substantive finding.** g = 0 holds exactly when draw = income − r_b·D: the operator **services the interest and withdraws the rest**. Everything II-4 computed is that policy's numbers. The neighbours are nowhere near it. Withdraw everything *including* the interest (draw = income) and g = r_b exactly, so ν_eff = 2.5% − 5% = **−2.5% < 0 and liquidation is certain at any leverage whatever** — the debt compounds at 5% against a price whose median grows at 2.5%, and only the dividend closes that gap, which a withdrawn dividend does not. Retain everything (draw = 0) and, on a saturated running example carrying a debit of 1.56 against 0.772/yr of unconstrained income, ν_eff goes strongly positive and liquidation becomes negligible. **Whether a levered wheel survives turns mostly on whether income is retained to service the debt** — a lever the operator controls, unlike γ_s.

  It also gives the third failure mode a criterion in the same currency as the other two — lots return iff ν > 0, their capital returns iff m > σ², the **account survives iff ν > g** — and it is what finally consumes `r_b` and `fin_spread`, declared in II-3 and unused until now.

  **What is approximate, stated plainly.** g is constant only when the net drain is proportional to the debit. The two policies above satisfy that exactly; a fixed cash draw does not, and for it g is a linearization around the current debit. Income is steady only once the account is saturated. Both are II-6's to bound, and the item's brief changed accordingly: the simulator now measures *where the linearization breaks* rather than *which way the debit drifts*, which was previously unknown.

  **The exponent is not a new constant, and that is the result worth stating.** First passage to a lower price barrier is governed by θ = 2ν/σ², the same tail exponent that decides whether the depth census's capital integral converges ([eq:capital-criterion](#eq:capital-criterion)). Both are the exponent of the exponential martingale of the same drifting walk, read once as the decay rate of a stationary tail and once as the decay rate of a hitting probability. `verify_examples.py` reads θ out of `criteria()` rather than recomputing it, so the two cannot drift apart.

  **The barrier recovers the broker's own ceiling instead of assuming it.** f\* ≥ 1 — a position in violation the day it is put on — happens exactly at L ≥ 1/γ_s. Checked at all three levered γ_s. The finite-horizon first passage collapses onto f\*^θ to 1e-12, and the L_max inversion round-trips through `liquidation_prob` to 1e-12, at every γ_s and both tolerances.

  **Amended 2026-07-29 — a latent crash in the reflection term, found the same day by the II-5 pre-flight and fixed.** `_log_ncdf` switched to its Mills-ratio asymptotic below z = −20, on the assumption that `NormalDist.cdf` underflows near the double-precision floor. ## Decided and descoped

Four things the article deliberately does not model. All four are named in
[the outlook](#sec:outlook), so the article itself is the guard against mistaking them for
oversights; the reasoning is kept here because it stays valid if any of them is ever revisited.

- 🚫 **The call-strike lever** (was #10; descoped 2026-07-26). K_c is frozen at entry, [the strategy section](#sec:strategy) says so explicitly, and the article's verdict is therefore about the *mechanical* wheel. The original entry, kept because the reasoning still stands: in practice the operator moves the call strike down to exit stuck lots (buying q at a realized-loss cost) or up in recoveries (extra gain, lower q). "Call strike vs. basis" is the operator's main tool against dead strata and the natural control knob of a policy layer. Empirically the lever is used asymmetrically — strikes move up freely, down only rarely — which poses a genuinely interesting question: is that reluctance optimal (the option value of waiting for recovery), or loss-aversion the model should advise against? And the question is now *tractable*: the first-passage machinery prices the wait (expected time and carry at depth x against the realized loss of striking down), so "optimal reluctance" is a computable threshold rather than a philosophical stance. This was flagged in 2026-07-22 as the single most consequential open item, on the grounds that without the lever the base-case excess return is ≈ 0 — which is exactly why descoping it is a statement about scope and not a shrug.
- 🚫 **Permanent-impairment hazard** (was #13, with #25; descoped 2026-07-27). "Fundamentally sound" fails occasionally in real portfolios: the lot enters an absorbing state with q = 0, premium ≈ 0, capital loss ≈ 100%, and even a small per-year hazard adds an expected cost that can rival annual premium income.

  **It has a derived entry point** (2026-07-26, from the dividend-stickiness review). The article's asset is a dividend aristocrat — a payout that rises and never falls — and [the strategy section](#sec:strategy) says so explicitly, because that clause is what licenses a constant δ. Push the clause to its limit and it names its own failure boundary. With the payout frozen in dollars and the total return held fixed, a lot at depth x faces a yield of δ·e^x on the market value of its shares, so the drag grows with depth and

        nu(x)  =  nu − δ·(e^x − 1),      zero at   x\* = ln(1 + nu/δ)

  changing sign at x\* and growing more negative beyond it — a runaway region, not a slow one, and the same object as the Gordon-model price at which a fixed payout stops being payable. At the running parameters x\* = 0.693 (**50% below the strike**), with 16% of the thirty-year census already past it; under the pricing drift x\* = 0.182 (**17% below**), with 69% of the census beyond. So the never-cut assumption is not merely optimistic in the deep tail, it is *inconsistent with option prices* there, and the impairment hazard is the mechanism that resolves the contradiction. `model.sticky_dividend_trap()` computes the boundary; `model.sticky_dividend_yield()` computes the bounded version of the correction the article does adopt (four basis points at thirty years, see [the returns section](#sec:returns)). Two consequences for whoever picks this up: the hazard is naturally *depth-dependent* rather than a flat per-lot rate, and x\* gives it a scale to calibrate against instead of a free parameter.

  **The empirical handle is gone** (2026-07-27, with the universe restatement), which is what closed the item — including the separate entry (was #25) that expected the hazard to accumulate observations. The live account's only two entries into names it would later disown, ALT and BEKE, were the sole observations; both are now out of universe, and rightly so — neither is a name the strategy would knowingly enter, so a rate estimated from them would describe a universe the article does not claim. Consequently **the impairment channel is untested and no rate can be quoted**, and the out-of-sample pre-registration's P12 has been *retired rather than passed*. Calibrating this needs an in-universe name that falls and stays down, which is a slower experiment than the rest of Part IV. Until then x\* is the only scale available, and it is theoretical.

  Rejected along the way, recorded so it is not re-invented: crediting a lot's dividend income by its own realized depth (income ∝ δ·e^x per lot). It is internally inconsistent — all shares of one company pay the same cash on the same day, so identical shares would receive different dividends — and the tell is that E[e^x] over the census *is* the cost-basis capital, so the correction is worth +1.22pp of pure unfunded return at 30y and breaks the no-arbitrage identity immediately. **Any dividend correction must move the drift and the income together.**
- 🚫 **Transaction-cost haircut as a function of tenor** (was #12; recorded in the outlook 2026-07-26). Commissions eat a few percent of premium on short-tenor cheap options against a fraction of a percent on monthly ones — and the running example is now firmly in the frictional regime. Without a friction term the model has no reason not to prefer ever-shorter tenors, so the haircut is a prerequisite for any τ_p/T optimization. It is not one, so the term stays out and the limitation is named.
- 🚫 **Volatility skew and the σ_IV surface** (was #4's second half, and #23; decided 2026-07-27). The article carries **one** implied volatility and **one** spread over realized, a single scalar `Config.iv_spread` for both legs, every strike and every lot depth. Skew means the puts sold are systematically richer than the calls sold, so the edge concentrates in the put leg and in shallow lots — which matters *more* since the move to a weekly cadence, not less: the break-even spread computed in [the returns section](#sec:returns) is essentially **zero**, so the whole of the edge is the spread, and *where* the spread sits decides who earns it. The decision rests on measurement rather than convenience: the put leg's spread over realized volatility runs ~2× the call leg's, and the within-name depth slope is **+30–40% relative IV** from shallow to deep (restated 2026-07-27 from +20%; the apparent effect is partly name selection). Splitting `iv_spread` into put and call legs is a small change; a strike- and depth-dependent surface is a renumber-every-figure job through Parts II–III and was judged not worth it. [The returns section](#sec:returns) names both simplifications and states the sign of the bias. **Residue:** the measured panel still has to be *reported* in Part IV — carried into TODO IV-2.

**The spine.** A lot's state is its depth x = ln(K_c/S); on the call grid x is a Gaussian random
walk with per-period drift −ν·τ_c; exit is the first grid point with x ≤ 0. Everything follows:
entry law for x₀ → q(x) as the one-step exit probability → grid-sampled first passage for the
holding time → Little's law E[I] = λ·E[T] → the stationary depth census → income and capital as

**The decisions that shaped it.** All eight are now binding through `CLAUDE.md` and
`sections/00-notation.md`; they are kept here as the record of *why*.

1. *One chain in ν.* Every formula is the same formula with a drift argument m; everything
   downstream depends on it only through ν = m − σ²/2. A "measure" is a value of ν:
   Q-world = (r−δ, σ_IV), P-world = (μ−δ, σ_RV). Both are self-consistent worlds and both are
   reported wherever they differ materially; P leads for anything the operator lives through,
   Q for anything the market quotes, r for Track C.
2. *Two criteria, not one.* Lot count is stable iff ν > 0; expected capital is stable iff
   m > σ² (a lot's basis relative to current spot is e^x, and E[1/S_t] must decay). At the
   running parameters the verdict flips between measures — the article's sharpest result.
3. *Two regimes, side by side.* Standard p\* = 20% (the prose thread), Conservative p\* = 10%.
   Neither is fitted to the live account, which falls between them.
4. *Three clocks.* Cadence T ≥ tenor τ_p, call τ_c. Little's law wants an arrival rate anyway

5. *Volatility risk premium off by default* (σ_IV = σ_RV): headline results describe the

6. *No strike-down lever*: K_c frozen at entry. The article's verdict is therefore a statement

7. *The d = 0.15 stress case dies* — depth is a process, not a parameter; the stress axis is σ.
