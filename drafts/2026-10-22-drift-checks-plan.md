# Drift checks for prose and the appendix — plan

**Status: agreed 2026-10-22**, in conversation, before any code. Two additions to the
apparatus `verify_examples.py` already runs.

## Why

The article has three kinds of content that can go stale, and only two were checked.

- **Quoted numbers** have a case in `code/examples/`, which recomputes them.
- **Generated files** — the figures — are redrawn and compared byte for byte.
- **Prose that restates or rests on another part of the article** has nothing. §02's
  contribution 5 said the wheel "clears in four years" for weeks after the cadence change
  made it two, "carried by nothing but prose, which is why no check caught it"
  (`DONE.md`). §04's "a reason nobody expects" contradicted §07 two sections later.

And one generated file had no check after all: the reproduction appendix went stale on
2026-09-15 and 2026-09-18 and nothing noticed until it was regenerated by hand (`e8861ba`).

## 1. The appendix is compared against a fresh run

The same treatment the figures get: `verify_examples.py` generates the appendix and
compares it with `sections/99-reproduction.md`, line endings aside. Shortcut:
`python -m examples --appendix-check`.

## 2. Signed claims

A **claim** is a paragraph whose statement rests on another part of the article. Signing
it records a fingerprint of both sides — the citing paragraph and what it rests on — in
`sections/claims.tsv`. When either side's wording changes, the check fails and names the
claim, so the paragraph is reread against its source and signed again. A signature means
someone read both sides; it is never applied in bulk.

**Targets must be precise**, or the check is noise. A whole section is a poor target: every
edit to §09's 8,570 words would flag every claim resting on any of it, and signatures
renewed in bulk certify nothing. So a claim rests on one of:

- a **displayed formula** (`{#eq:}`) — the display line;
- a **figure** (`{#fig:}`) — its image line;
- a **heading with an anchor** — that heading's section or subsection;
- a **paragraph anchor** — one paragraph, or one list item.

**Paragraph anchors** are new: `<a id="para:name"></a>`, on its own line directly above the
paragraph (no blank line between), or just after a list item's marker. On GitHub it renders
as an invisible anchor; at assembly a pandoc filter turns it into a PDF target (below). Prose
may link one like any anchor, so a back-reference can land on the paragraph it means rather
than at the top of a section. They are not numbered, so §00's registers do not list them.

**Not every link is a claim.** Most section links are pointers ("the stability section asks
when it is finite"), and only claims are signed. Which links are claims lives in
`claims.tsv`, not in the prose, which stays clean.

**The citing side** is named in `claims.tsv` either as a file — meaning the paragraphs in it
that link the target — or by a paragraph anchor, for a paragraph that restates a result
without linking it, as §02's list does.

**Commands:** `python -m examples --claims` checks; `--sign CITING TARGET` signs one claim;
`--unsign CITING TARGET` drops one.

## 3. Assembly

Pandoc drops raw HTML when writing LaTeX, so without a filter a paragraph anchor vanishes
from the PDF. This filter, tested with pandoc 3.1.3, makes it a `\hypertarget` and a prose
link to it a `\hyperlink`; it belongs to the assembly step when that is built:

```lua
function RawInline(el)
  if el.format == "html" then
    local id = el.text:match('^<a id="([^"]+)">$')
    if id then return pandoc.Span({}, pandoc.Attr(id)) end
    if el.text == "</a>" then return {} end
  end
end
```

## Pilot

Signed first: the claims that restate other sections — §02's list of what the article
answers, and back-references such as §10's "as the inventory section warned". Other claims
are signed as the section review reaches them, starting with §09, so each signature comes
from an actual reading.
