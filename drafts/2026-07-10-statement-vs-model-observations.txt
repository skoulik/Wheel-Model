# Real Statement vs. Model: Observations from a Year of Live Wheel Trading

Source: statements/USD.csv (private, gitignored) — an Interactive Brokers cash-flow
statement covering roughly 2025-05 through 2026-05. Parsed: 1,064 closed option
positions (plus 65 still open), 142 stock transactions. Analysis excluded a "junk"
universe (~30 low-priced legacy/meme/bankruptcy tickers: BLNK, FOSL, BYND, PLUG, NIO,
MPW, CLOV, ...) identified by share price < $8; those positions are mostly covered
calls used to liquidate dead legacy stock, plus bankruptcy remnants — interesting as a
cautionary tale (see #9) but not wheel data. The remaining "quality" universe is ~40
names (ABT, ACN, ADP, BMY, CLX, CMCSA, ELV, GPC, HPQ, KMB, LOW, MDT, MRK, MSFT, NVO,
PFE, PYPL, SLB, TGT, UNH, ZTS, ...): 703 closed puts, 233 closed calls, 34 completed
inventory lots, 24 lots still open.

Headline: the model's *architecture* (queue of lots, put arrivals, call departures,
self-recycling) matches the practice well. The parameter conventions and several
simplifying assumptions do not. Numbered findings below, each with the model
implication.

---

## 1. The duty-cycle gap: option tenor ≠ cadence period  [biggest structural miss]

The model assumes one put always running: period = option lifetime τ_p. Reality: the
dominant pattern (496 of 703 puts) is a **2–4 day option sold on a weekly cadence** —
sold Tuesday (525 of 703 opens are Tuesdays), expiring Friday. The stock is "covered"
by a live put only ~40–50% of calendar time.

Model implication: introduce two separate parameters — cadence period T (how often a
put is sold) and tenor τ_p ≤ T (how long it lives). Premium accrues once per T but is
priced off τ_p; assignment probability per cadence period is that of a τ_p-day option,
which for 3 days is far smaller than a weekly-tenor put. All annualizations change by
the factor τ_p/T. The current model is the special case τ_p = T.

## 2. Realized put assignment rate is ~8–9%, not ~18–20%

Across 703 quality puts: 57 assigned = 8.1% (weekly bucket 8.7%, monthly bucket 12.9%).
The article's running example targets p ≈ 17.6%. The operator in practice runs much
further out of the money (median premium 0.26% of strike on 3-day puts) — and the
risk-neutral→real-world gap (p_rw < p) plus put skew push realized frequency down
further. Empirically validates the draft's conservatism claim, but the *examples*
should feature a low-p* regime (p* ≈ 5–10%) as the realistic base case, with p* = 20%
as the aggressive variant.

## 3. Recycling is much faster than the stress example — for lots that recycle

Realized call-away rate per call position: 29% for ≤1-week calls, ~16% for monthly
calls. Note the model's *stress* example (d = 0.15) gives q ≈ 16% per QUARTER; reality
shows ~16% per MONTH. Median completed-lot holding time: 27 days; median call tenor 17
days (so realized n = τ_c/τ_p ≈ 3–6 against weekly puts). Consistent with the derived
E[d | assignment] ≈ 8% picture (TODO #3), not with d = 0.15 as base case.

## 4. Dead strata are real, visible, and censored out of naive statistics  [tier 2 in the flesh]

While completed lots recycle in a median of 27 days (max 161), the OPEN inventory tells
the tier-2 story: 24 unresolved lots, 10 of them older than 200 days — BEKE 384 days,
NVO 344, HRL 314, a cluster of STRF lots at 110–115 (230–324 days), PYPL, ZTS, ADP ×2,
MSFT @ 370, UNH @ 260. Capital sits precisely where the homogeneous model says it
shouldn't: in old, high-strike layers that no longer recycle.

Two model implications:
  a) **Survivorship/censoring**: statistics computed on completed lots (median 27d)
     wildly overstate the true exit rate; the stuck tail is right-censored. Any
     empirical calibration of q must handle censoring (survival analysis, not sample
     averages of completed spells).
  b) The distribution of holding times is heavy-tailed: most lots exit in ~1 month,
     a persistent minority effectively never do. A single q cannot produce this; a
     depth-dependent q_i (tier 2) or a two-regime mixture (fast lane / trapped) can.
     The mixture might be the cleaner tier-2 parameterization.

## 5. The call-strike policy is an active lever, not a constant

Model: call strike = original put strike, always. Reality (calls matched to held
inventory): 102 at basis, 26 above basis, 16 below basis; of 34 completed lots, 26
exited at entry strike, 6 above (strike improvement — extra capital gain), 2 below
(deliberate exit at a realized loss, e.g. KVUE assigned at 20, called away at 18).
The operator manages stuck lots by moving the call strike down (raising q, giving up
basis) or up in recoveries (raising gain, lowering q).

Model implication: K_c becomes a policy variable; "call strike vs. basis" is the
natural control knob in the tier-2 phase diagram — it is exactly the operator's tool
for preventing dead strata, at a quantifiable realized-loss cost. This deserves to be
a first-class part of tier 2, arguably the operator's main degree of freedom.

## 6. Arrivals are cross-sectionally clustered (common shocks)

Assignments cluster on dates: 2026-04-25 alone assigned ABT, ACN, MDT and TSCO;
2026-03-28 assigned MSFT and UNH. Running ~40 concurrent wheels diversifies
idiosyncratic moves (supporting the "portfolio" resolution of TODO #1 for exits), but
arrivals are driven by market-wide drawdowns and land in bursts. A portfolio-level
model should treat p as having a common systematic component — arrival bursts are
exactly when capital is scarcest (reflexivity, section 09, now with direct evidence).

## 7. Transaction costs are not negligible at weekly cadence

Commission on the dominant weekly puts: median 3.6% of premium collected (cheap
options, fixed per-contract fees). On monthly puts: ~0.1–0.2%. Assignment/delivery
commissions are trivial (cents). Model implication: a friction haircut on c_p that
grows as tenor shrinks — it directly counteracts the higher annualized premium of
short cadences and belongs in any τ_p optimization (the model currently has no reason
not to prefer ever-shorter tenors).

## 8. Early management exists but is minor

~4% of puts and calls are bought back before expiry (puts typically repurchased at
~11% of the premium received — locking profit and freeing margin early). A refinement,
not a structural gap; note it and move on.

## 9. The "fundamentally sound" assumption fails in the field — and has an absorbing state

The junk universe (excluded above) is a graveyard: covered calls at $1–$3 strikes
grinding out exits from collapsed legacy positions, bankruptcy remnants (FibroGen
contra rights, Walgreens tender odd lots, reverse-split fragments). Once a stock
collapses permanently, its lot exits the wheel mathematics entirely: q = 0 forever,
premium ≈ 0, capital loss ~100%. The model's asset-quality assumption hides a
tail term: even a small per-year probability of permanent impairment adds a
non-recycling absorbing state whose expected cost can rival the annual premium
income. Worth an explicit term in tier 2 (a per-lot hazard of "death") rather than
a verbal disclaimer — the statement shows real portfolios accumulate these.

## 10. Encouraging confirmations

- Call premium contributed 62% as much gross income as put premium ($17.5k vs $28k in
  closed quality positions) — strikingly close to the corrected model's decomposition,
  where the call term p·c_c/q is ~63% of the put term in the section-08 stress example.
- Median monthly-put premium 0.72% of strike vs. the model's Black–Scholes 0.5% at
  k = 0.95 — the right ballpark, gap consistent with IV > RV plus skew (TODO #4).
- Exit-at-entry-strike is indeed the dominant mode (26 of 34) — the self-recycling
  accounting (gain = original premium) describes the majority of completed cycles.
- The operator sells puts continuously while holding inventory, including on the same
  names (layering), exactly as the queue model assumes.
